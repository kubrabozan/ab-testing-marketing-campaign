Did the ad actually work?

An A/B test on 588,101 users. The question: does showing a product ad make people buy, compared with a public-service announcement shown in the same slot?

+43% lift

Conversion rose from 1.79% to 2.55%. Three different methods give the same answer.

SIGNIFICANT · p < 0.0001

01

The question

A company ran a campaign. Most users saw a product advertisement. A small hold-out group (exactly 4%) saw a public-service announcement in the same slot. Everything else was the same, so any difference in purchases can be credited to the ad.

H₀: both groups convert at the same rate. H₁: they don't (two-sided, because we'd also want to know if the ad hurt). α = 0.05.

02

The headline result

The ad group converted at 2.55%, against 1.79% for the control. The 95% confidence interval for the uplift is 0.60 – 0.94 pp, and it doesn't come close to zero.

Conversion rate by group, with 95% confidence intervals. The control interval is wider because the group is 24× smaller.
03

Three ways of asking the same question

With only 420 conversions in the control group, I didn't want the conclusion to depend on a single method. So I tested it three ways.

Frequentist

z = 7.37

A two-proportion z-test. The chi-square test gives the same p-value, as it should for a 2×2 table (χ² = z²).

Bootstrap

+31% – +58%

A 95% interval for the relative lift from 10,000 resamples, with no normal approximation needed.

Bayesian

≈100%

The posterior probability that the ad beats the PSA, using a Beta-Binomial model with a flat prior.

Bootstrap distribution of the relative lift. The red line is "no effect", and none of the 10,000 resamples reach it.
04

The ad needs repetition

Splitting users by how many ads they saw shows something the headline hides. For users who saw 1–10 ads, the ad did nothing. The control group even converted slightly better. The gap only opens above about 20 ads.

The campaign works, but only once people have seen it many times.

A caveat: exposure is measured after assignment, and heavy platform users see more ads and buy more. So this pattern is descriptive, not causal. It's a strong lead for a follow-up experiment that randomises ad frequency.

Conversion rate by total ads seen. Both groups rise with exposure; the gap between them is the ad's contribution.
05

When does it work? (Correcting for multiple tests)

Testing all seven weekdays means seven chances of a false positive, which adds up to about a 30% risk at α = 0.05. After the Holm correction, five days stay significant. Thursday and Sunday don't, but with only about 3,000 control users per day, that means "not enough evidence", not "no effect".

Relative lift by the weekday each user saw the most ads. Filled dots are significant after the Holm correction.
06

Was the test big enough?

With a 96/4 split, the small control group limits statistical power. Even so, the smallest uplift the test could reliably detect (80% power) was 0.25 pp, about a third of what we observed. A 50/50 split would have needed only about 90k users. But it would have shown the ad to far fewer people, so keeping a small hold-out is a sensible business trade-off.

07

Recommendation

Roll out the campaign, then test ad frequency next.

  • About 770 extra purchases per 100,000 users, or about 4,300 purchases caused by this campaign.
  • The effect appears only at higher frequency, so a frequency experiment could show where extra impressions stop adding value.
  • Combine this with cost-per-impression and margin data to confirm the campaign is profitable, not just effective.
