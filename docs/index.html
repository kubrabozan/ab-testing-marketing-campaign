Should we keep paying for this ad?

The marketing team has to decide whether to renew a campaign budget. An A/B test on 588,101 users answers 3 questions: does the ad work, is it worth the money and where does it work?

The data

Most users (96%) were shown a product advertisement. A hold-out group (4%) saw a public-service announcement in the same slot. Because the PSA fills the same space, any difference in purchases can be credited to the ad itself, not just to having an ad there.

03
BQ1

Does the ad cause more purchases?

Conversion rate by group, with 95% confidence intervals. The control interval is wider because that group is 24× smaller.
Answer: yesConversion rises from 1.79% to 2.55%. That's about 4,343 purchases that wouldn't have happened without the ad (95% CI 3,360 to 5,326). If the ad did nothing, random luck in who landed in which group would produce a gap this large less than 1 in a trillion times. In other words, this is not a fluke.
04

How sure are we?

People only act on a number they trust. With just 420 purchases in the control group, I didn't want the answer to depend on 1 method, so I checked it 3 ways.

Frequentist

z = 7.37

A two-proportion z-test. The chi-square test gives the same p-value (χ² = z²).

Bootstrap

+31% to +58%

A 95% interval for the lift from 10,000 resamples, with no normal approximation needed.

Bayesian

>99.99%

Probability that the ad beats the PSA (Beta-Binomial, flat prior).

Bootstrap distribution of the relative lift. The red line is "no effect" and none of the 10,000 resamples reach it.

Was the test big enough? The observed effect is 3.1× the smallest effect this design could reliably detect. A 50/50 split would have needed fewer users, but it would have hidden the ad from 50% of the audience. The 4% hold-out was the right trade-off.

05
BQ2

Is it worth the money?

The data has no prices, so instead of guessing them I asked: at what price does the campaign pay off? There is no ad log either, but each user row says how many ads that user saw. Adding those up, the variant group was shown 14.0M ads, which produced 0.31 extra purchases per 1,000 ads shown. Ads are priced per 1,000 ads shown (this price is called CPM), so that converts directly into a cost per extra purchase:

CPMCampaign costCost per extra purchase
Answer: likely and 1 number decides itAt a typical $5 CPM, each extra purchase costs about $16 in ads. If a sale earns more profit than that, the campaign pays for itself. That turns an open question into a single check with the finance team.

Measuring cost something too. The hold-out group "lost" about 181 purchases by seeing the PSA, under 5% of what the campaign generated. That's a small price for knowing the campaign works at all.

06
BQ3

Where does it work?

Frequency is the biggest lever. Users who saw 20 or fewer ads show no lift (−3%, p = 0.78). Above 20 views, the lift is +56%.

2 in 3 users saw too few ads to be moved and they used up about 21% of the budget.

This isn't proof that capping frequency would save 21%: heavy platform users see more ads and may simply be keener buyers. But it is the most valuable thing to test next, because it's the biggest potential saving.

Conversion rate by total ads seen. The gap between the bars is the ad's contribution and it only opens after about 20 views.

Should budget move between weekdays? Testing 7 days means 7 chances of a false positive, so I used the Holm correction. 5 days stay significant. Thursday and Sunday don't, because there are only about 3,000 control users per day.

Relative lift by the weekday each user saw the most ads. Filled dots are significant after the Holm correction.
Answer: don't reallocate by day"Most ads day" is when users saw ads, not when they bought. That's too weak a basis for moving money. Knowing what the data does not support is part of the answer.
07

Recommendation

Keep funding the campaign and test frequency before the next budget cycle

  1. Renew the budget. The ad causes about 4,343 extra purchases and 3 methods agree.
  2. Confirm profit per sale with finance. Above about $16 (at a $5 CPM), it's profitable.
  3. Run a frequency-cap experiment (e.g. 10 / 20 / 40 ads). About 21% of ads shown had no measurable effect today.
  4. Keep a small hold-out in every campaign. It's the only reason any of this can be measured.
  5. Don't reallocate by weekday. The evidence doesn't support it.

TakeawayThe ad works and likely pays for itself, but about 21% of the spend may be wasted on users who don't see it often enough. The next step is to test frequency, not to spend more.
